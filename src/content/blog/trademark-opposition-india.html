After a trademark application is accepted, it is advertised in the Trade Marks Journal. That opens a window in which any person can oppose it. Whether you want to stop a conflicting mark or you are defending your own application, the deadlines are strict. This guide walks through the process under Section 21 of the Trade Marks Act, 1999 and rules 42 to 50 of the Trade Marks Rules, 2017.

Step 1: Notice of opposition (four months)

Any person may file a notice of opposition within four months from the date of the advertisement or re-advertisement of the application (Section 21(1), as amended in 2010; rule 42). The notice is filed in Form TM-O with the official fee for each class opposed.

The four-month period was set by the 2010 amendment, which removed the earlier provision for extending the opposition period. Watching the Journal for marks similar to yours is the practical way not to miss it.

The notice must identify the application opposed, the goods or services concerned, and the earlier mark or right relied on, with its details (rule 43). Grounds commonly include those in Sections 9 and 11, such as similarity to an earlier mark or lack of distinctiveness. Our article on Section 9 and Section 11 explains these grounds.

Step 2: Counter-statement (two months)

The Registrar serves the notice on the applicant. The applicant must file a counter-statement in Form TM-O within two months from receiving the copy of the notice (Section 21(2); rule 44). If the applicant does not do so, the application is deemed abandoned.

Evidence is given by affidavit, with copies delivered to the other side. If the opponent takes no action within its two months, the opposition is deemed abandoned (rule 45(2)). No further evidence is allowed except with the Registrar's leave (rule 48). Documents in a language other than Hindi or English need an attested translation (rule 49).

Step 4: Hearing and decision

  • After evidence closes, the Registrar notifies the first hearing date, at least one month after the notice (rule 50(1)).
  • A party may ask for an adjournment with reasonable cause at least three days before the hearing; no party gets more than two adjournments, each of not more than thirty days (rule 50(2)).
  • If the applicant does not appear, the application may be treated as abandoned; if the opponent does not appear, the opposition may be dismissed for want of prosecution (rule 50(3) and (4)).
  • The Registrar considers written arguments and decides, communicating the decision in writing (rule 50(5) and (6)). After hearing the parties and considering the evidence, the Registrar decides whether, and subject to what conditions or limitations, the mark is to be registered (Section 21(5)).

Tips for opponents

  • Monitor the Trade Marks Journal regularly for marks similar to yours.
  • Keep evidence of your use, sales, advertising and reputation ready, arranged by date.
  • Be clear about the earlier rights relied on and the goods or services overlap.

Tips for applicants

  • Note the two-month counter-statement deadline as soon as the notice arrives; missing it means the application is deemed abandoned.
  • Gather evidence of your adoption and use of the mark early.
  • Consider whether a negotiated settlement, such as limiting your goods or services, is better than a contested hearing.

Frequently asked questions

Who can oppose a trademark?

Any person can file a notice of opposition under Section 21(1); it is not limited to owners of registered marks.

Can the four-month opposition period be extended?

No. Since the 2010 amendment, Section 21(1) sets four months from advertisement without a provision for extension.

What if no one opposes?

If the application is not opposed within the period, or an opposition is decided in the applicant's favour, the mark proceeds to registration under Section 23.
