Novelty, inventive step, industrial applicability and the Section 3 exclusions, explained in plain language. category: patent ---

Not every new idea can be patented. Under the Patents Act, 1970, an invention must meet three tests and must not fall within the list of things the Act excludes. Understanding these criteria before you file saves time and money, and helps you draft stronger claims.

The legal definition of an invention

Section 2(1)(j) defines an "invention" as a new product or process involving an inventive step and capable of industrial application. That gives three tests: novelty, inventive step and industrial applicability.

1. Novelty

An invention is new if it has not been anticipated by publication in any document, or used in India or anywhere else in the world, before the date of filing of the application with the complete specification (Section 2(1)(l)). Prior art is worldwide: a journal article in another country, an earlier patent anywhere, or a product already sold can all destroy novelty.

This is why a prior art search before filing is so useful, and why you should file before disclosing the invention publicly.

The Act contains limited exceptions (Sections 29 to 34). For example, displaying an invention at an exhibition notified by the Central Government, or describing it in a paper read before a learned society, does not by itself destroy novelty, provided the application is filed within 12 months and the other conditions are met. These exceptions are narrow, so it is safer not to rely on them.

2. Inventive step

Under Section 2(1)(ja), an inventive step is a feature that involves a technical advance compared with existing knowledge, or has economic significance, or both, and that makes the invention not obvious to a person skilled in the art.

In practice, examiners compare the invention with the closest prior art and ask whether a skilled person would have arrived at it without inventive effort. Combining known elements in an obvious way, or making routine changes, usually fails this test.

3. Industrial applicability

An invention is capable of industrial application if it can be made or used in an industry (Section 2(1)(ac)). Purely theoretical ideas, or inventions that cannot work, fail this test.

What cannot be patented: Section 3

Even if an invention meets the three tests, it cannot be patented if it falls under Section 3. The exclusions include:

  • frivolous inventions or those contrary to well-established natural laws (Section 3(a));
  • inventions contrary to public order or morality, or seriously prejudicial to health or the environment (Section 3(b));
  • the mere discovery of a scientific principle or abstract theory, or of a living or non-living substance occurring in nature (Section 3(c));
  • the mere discovery of a new form of a known substance that does not enhance its known efficacy (Section 3(d));
  • a mere admixture giving only the aggregate of the properties of its components (Section 3(e));
  • methods of agriculture or horticulture (Section 3(h));
  • processes for the medicinal, surgical or other treatment of human beings or animals (Section 3(i));
  • plants and animals, other than micro-organisms (Section 3(j));
  • a mathematical or business method, a computer programme per se, or algorithms (Section 3(k));
  • literary, dramatic, musical or artistic works (Section 3(l));
  • a mere scheme, rule or method of performing a mental act or playing a game (Section 3(m));
  • presentation of information (Section 3(n));
  • traditional knowledge, or an aggregation or duplication of known properties of traditionally known components (Section 3(p)).

Inventions relating to atomic energy falling under Section 20(1) of the Atomic Energy Act, 1962 are also not patentable (Section 4).

A quick self-check before filing

  1. Is it a product or a process, not just an idea, a business plan or a discovery?
  2. Has anything identical been published, sold or used anywhere in the world?
  3. What is the technical advance over the closest existing solution, and would it be obvious to an expert?
  4. Can it be made or used in an industry?
  5. Does it fall within any Section 3 or Section 4 exclusion?

Frequently asked questions

Is software patentable in India?

A computer programme per se is excluded under Section 3(k). An invention that produces a technical effect, with software as one part of it, may be patentable depending on how it is claimed.

Does novelty mean new in India only?

No. Novelty is judged against worldwide prior art.

Can a known medicine be patented in a new form?

Only if the new form meets Section 3(d), which generally requires enhanced efficacy compared with the known substance, as well as the other tests.
