Before you spend time and money on a patent application, it pays to check whether your invention is actually new. A patent search, also called a prior art or novelty search, looks for earlier patents, publications and products that disclose the same idea. This guide explains why it matters and how to run a basic search yourself.

Why search before filing?

Under Section 2(1)(l) of the Patents Act, 1970, an invention is new only if it has not been anticipated by publication in any document, or used in India or anywhere else in the world, before the filing date. Prior art is worldwide and includes any language.

A search helps you:

  • avoid filing for something that is already known;
  • identify what is genuinely different about your invention, so the claims can focus on it;
  • anticipate the documents an examiner may cite in the First Examination Report;
  • spot patents you might infringe (a separate freedom-to-operate question).

Do not stop at patents. Research papers, product manuals, websites, conference talks and videos can all be prior art.

A step-by-step search method

  1. Describe the invention in one or two sentences: the problem, the solution and the key features.
  2. List keywords and synonyms: technical and everyday terms, abbreviations, and the words a competitor might use.
  3. Find the right classification: look up the International Patent Classification (IPC) or Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) codes for your field and search within them. Classification catches documents that use different words.
  4. Search in rounds: start broad, then combine keywords with classification codes and narrow down.
  5. Follow citations: for each close document, check the documents it cites and the later documents that cite it.
  6. Compare feature by feature: make a table of your key features against the closest documents. If one document shows every feature, the invention is not new. If several documents together suggest it, inventive step may be an issue.
  7. Record what you did: keep the search strings, databases and dates. This helps later drafting and any FER response.

Limits of a do-it-yourself search

  • Applications are generally published about 18 months after their priority date, so recent filings may not be visible yet.
  • Free tools may miss documents in other languages or older documents that are poorly indexed.
  • No search can prove an invention is new; it reduces the risk of surprises.

A professional search adds classification expertise, wider coverage and a written report that compares your features with the closest prior art.

Keep it confidential

Searching public databases does not disclose your invention, but describing it publicly does. Do not publish, present or sell before filing. If you need to discuss it with others, use a non-disclosure agreement.

Frequently asked questions

If I find nothing similar, can I be sure of getting a patent?

No. A clear search improves your position, but grant depends on examination of novelty, inventive step, industrial applicability and the Section 3 exclusions. See our article on patentability criteria.

If I find something similar, should I give up?

Not necessarily. If your invention differs in a way that solves a problem better, it may still be patentable with well-focused claims.

Is a patent search the same as a freedom-to-operate search?

No. A novelty search asks whether your invention is new. A freedom-to-operate search asks whether making or selling your product would infringe someone else's patent in force in a particular country.
