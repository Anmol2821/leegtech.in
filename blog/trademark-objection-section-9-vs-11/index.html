Trademark Objection Reply: Section 9 vs Section 11 Objections

By Anmol Shrivastava, Founder, Leegtech · Published 28 November 2026 · 4 min read

Most trademark applications in India receive an examination report raising objections before they are accepted. The two most common types are objections under Section 9 and Section 11 of the Trade Marks Act, 1999. They raise different issues and need different replies. This guide explains both, the deadline and how to respond.

The examination report and the deadline

Under rule 33 of the Trade Marks Rules, 2017, the Registrar examines the application, including a search of earlier marks for the same or similar goods or services. Any objection is sent to the applicant as an examination report.

If the applicant does not respond within one month from receiving the report, the Registrar may treat the application as abandoned (rule 33(4)). If the reply is not satisfactory, or the applicant asks for one, a hearing is offered (rule 33(6)).

Section 9: absolute grounds

Section 9 objections are about the mark itself, regardless of anyone else's mark. Under Section 9(1), a mark is not registrable if it:

  • is devoid of distinctive character, meaning it cannot distinguish your goods or services from others';
  • consists exclusively of words or signs that describe the kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, value, geographical origin, time of production or other characteristics of the goods or services; or
  • consists exclusively of words or signs that have become customary in the current language or in established trade practice.

Section 9(2) also bars marks that are likely to deceive or cause confusion, hurt religious susceptibilities, contain scandalous or obscene matter, or whose use is prohibited under the Emblems and Names (Prevention of Improper Use) Act, 1950. Section 9(3) bars certain shapes, such as a shape necessary to obtain a technical result.

How to reply to a Section 9 objection

  • Explain why the mark is distinctive: for example, that it is an invented or arbitrary word, or that it only suggests, rather than directly describes, the goods or services.
  • Look at the mark as a whole: a combination of words, or of words and a device, can be distinctive even if one part is descriptive.
  • Show acquired distinctiveness: under the proviso to Section 9(1), a mark is not refused if, before the application date, it acquired a distinctive character through use or is a well-known trade mark. Evidence of use, such as sales, advertising and invoices, usually goes in an affidavit.
  • Consider a disclaimer or amendment: for example, disclaiming exclusive rights in a descriptive word, where that is acceptable to you.

Section 11: relative grounds

Section 11 objections arise because of an earlier trade mark. Under Section 11(1), a mark is not registered if, because of its identity or similarity with an earlier mark and the identity or similarity of the goods or services, there is a likelihood of confusion on the part of the public, including the likelihood of association with the earlier mark.

An "earlier trade mark" includes a registered mark or an application with an earlier filing or priority date, and a well-known trade mark (Explanation to Section 11, as amended in 2010). Section 11 also protects well-known marks against use on dissimilar goods in certain cases (Section 11(2)).

Importantly, under Section 11(5), the Registrar may not refuse a mark on the grounds in Section 11(2) and 11(3) (well-known marks on dissimilar goods, passing off and copyright) unless the proprietor of the earlier mark raises them in opposition. Examination reports usually cite Section 11(1).

How to reply to a Section 11 objection

  • Compare the marks as a whole: visual, phonetic and conceptual differences, and the overall impression on an ordinary consumer.
  • Compare the goods or services: different nature, purpose, trade channels or buyers can reduce the likelihood of confusion.
  • Check the cited marks' status: a cited application may have been abandoned or refused, or the cited registration may have lapsed.
  • Narrow the specification: deleting or limiting goods or services can remove the overlap.
  • Consent or honest concurrent use: the earlier proprietor's consent (Section 11(4)), or honest concurrent use or other special circumstances (Section 12), can support registration.

Section 9 vs Section 11 at a glance

Section 9 (absolute)Section 11 (relative)
What it is aboutThe mark itself: distinctiveness, descriptiveness, deception and similar issuesConflict with an earlier trade mark
Typical evidenceArguments on meaning; evidence of use for acquired distinctivenessComparison of marks and goods; status of cited marks; consent or concurrent use
Common fixesDisclaimer, adding distinctive elements (by a new application if the change is substantial)Limiting goods or services, consent of the earlier proprietor

Note that under rule 37, an application cannot be amended in a way that substantially alters the trade mark or adds goods or services not in the original application.

Frequently asked questions

Is an examination report a refusal?

No. It lists objections. A well-reasoned reply, and a hearing where needed, gives the applicant the chance to overcome them.

What happens after a successful reply?

The Registrar accepts the application and it is advertised in the Trade Marks Journal (rule 33(5)), which opens a four-month window for opposition.

Can I avoid objections at the start?

Choosing a distinctive mark and running a trademark search before filing reduces the risk. See also our guide on choosing the right class.
