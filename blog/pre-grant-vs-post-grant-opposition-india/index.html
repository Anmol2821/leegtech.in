Pre-grant vs Post-grant Patent Opposition in India

By Anmol Shrivastava, Founder, Leegtech · Published 25 November 2026 · 4 min read

The Patents Act, 1970 lets third parties challenge a patent at two stages at the Patent Office: before grant, once the application is published, and after grant, within one year of the grant being published. Both are under Section 25, but they differ in who can file, when, and how the case is decided. This guide compares the two.

At a glance

Pre-grant oppositionPost-grant opposition
ProvisionSection 25(1), rule 55Section 25(2), rules 55A to 62
Who can fileAny personOnly a person interested
WhenAfter the application is published and before the patent is grantedAfter grant, within one year from the date of publication of the grant
FormForm 7A, with a copy to the applicantForm 7, in duplicate
Who decidesThe ControllerThe Controller, after a recommendation from an Opposition Board
Possible outcomesRepresentation rejected, specification amended, or grant refusedPatent maintained, amended or revoked

Grounds of opposition

The grounds are essentially the same at both stages, and only these grounds can be raised:

  • the invention was wrongfully obtained from the opponent;
  • a claim was published before its priority date in an Indian patent specification or in any other document in India or elsewhere (prior publication);
  • a claim is claimed in an earlier-dated Indian application published on or after the applicant's priority date (prior claiming);
  • the invention was publicly known or publicly used in India before the priority date;
  • the invention is obvious and clearly lacks an inventive step;
  • it is not an invention, or is not patentable, under the Act (including Section 3);
  • the complete specification does not sufficiently and clearly describe the invention or how to perform it;
  • the applicant failed to give, or gave false, information about corresponding foreign applications under Section 8;
  • a convention application was not filed within 12 months of the first filing in a convention country;
  • the specification does not disclose, or wrongly mentions, the source or geographical origin of biological material used;
  • the invention is anticipated by knowledge, oral or otherwise, available within any local or indigenous community in India or elsewhere.

The tests behind several of these grounds are explained in our article on patentability criteria in India.

How pre-grant opposition works

  1. Filing: the representation is filed in Form 7A with a statement, any evidence, and a request for hearing if wanted (rule 55(1)).
  2. When it is considered: only once a request for examination of the application has been filed (rule 55(2)). No patent is granted until six months after the application is published (rule 55(1A)).
  3. Prima facie review: since the 2024 amendment to rule 55(3), the Controller first decides whether a prima facie case is made out. If not, the opponent is notified and the representation is refused unless the opponent asks to be heard. If a prima facie case is made out, the applicant is notified.
  4. Applicant's reply: the applicant may file a statement and evidence within two months of the notice (rule 55(4), as amended in 2024).
  5. Decision: after considering both sides and hearing the parties if requested, the Controller rejects the representation, requires amendment, or refuses the application, by a speaking order (rule 55(5)).

The 2024 amendments also provide that an application in which a pre-grant representation has been filed and notice issued is examined under the expedited procedure of rule 24C (rule 55(5B)).

How post-grant opposition works

  1. Notice: a person interested files a notice of opposition in Form 7 (rule 55A), with a written statement setting out their interest, the facts and the relief sought, and any evidence, copied to the patentee (rule 57).
  2. Opposition Board: the Controller constitutes a three-member Opposition Board; the examiner who handled the application cannot be a member (rule 56).
  3. Patentee's reply: the patentee files a reply statement and evidence within two months of receiving the opponent's statement. If the patentee does not reply in time, the patent is deemed to be revoked (rule 58).
  4. Opponent's reply evidence: within one month, strictly confined to matters in the patentee's evidence (rule 59).
  5. Board's recommendation: the Board reports with reasons on each ground; the 2024 amendment set its time limit at two months from receiving the documents (rule 56(4)).
  6. Hearing and decision: after hearing both parties, the Controller orders that the patent be maintained, amended or revoked (Section 25(4)).

Which route fits?

  • Pre-grant is open to anyone, including those without a direct commercial interest, and can stop a patent before it is granted.
  • Post-grant needs a "person interested", which under Section 2(1)(t) includes a person engaged in, or promoting, research in the same field as the invention. It runs as a more formal proceeding with an Opposition Board.
  • After the one-year post-grant window, a patent can still be challenged by a petition for revocation, or a counter-claim in an infringement suit, before the High Court under Section 64.

Patent applicants should also watch for pre-grant oppositions after publication and be ready to respond within the time allowed.

Frequently asked questions

Can the same person file both a pre-grant and a post-grant opposition?

The Act does not prevent it, but a person interested who wants to oppose after grant must do so within one year from publication of the grant.

Is there an official fee?

Yes, official fees apply to both forms and depend on the category of the person filing; see our Official Fee Calculator.
