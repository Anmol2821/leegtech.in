Can Software Be Patented in India? Section 3(k) and the CRI Guidelines 2025

By Anmol Shrivastava, Founder, Leegtech · Published 25 November 2026 · 4 min read

"Software cannot be patented in India" is one of the most common misunderstandings about Indian patent law. The real position is more nuanced. A computer programme as such is excluded, but an invention implemented through software can be patentable if it solves a technical problem and produces a technical effect. This guide explains Section 3(k) of the Patents Act, 1970 and how the Indian Patent Office applies it under its Guidelines for Examination of Computer Related Inventions (CRIs), 2025.

What Section 3(k) says

Section 3(k) provides that the following are not inventions: "a mathematical or business method or a computer programme per se or algorithms". That gives four categories: mathematical methods, business methods, computer programmes per se, and algorithms.

The words "per se" apply to computer programmes. The CRI Guidelines 2025 explain that this means the exclusion is not absolute for every computer programme-led invention; it leaves room for inventions ancillary to or developed on a programme.

The key test: technical effect

Indian courts have repeatedly focused on whether a software-led invention makes a technical contribution or produces a technical effect:

  • In Ferid Allani v. Union of India (Delhi High Court, 12 December 2019), the court said that if an invention demonstrates a technical effect or a technical contribution, it is patentable even though it may be based on a computer program.
  • In Raytheon Company v. Controller General of Patents and Designs (Delhi High Court, 15 September 2023), the court held that requiring novel hardware is a higher standard that lacks any basis in law.

The CRI Guidelines 2025 adopt this approach. They state that allowability under Section 3(k) does not require novel hardware. A technical solution to a technical problem, through technical means, achieving a technical effect beyond a mere incidental effect, may take an invention outside the "computer programme per se" exclusion even when it is implemented by a computer programme.

How examiners assess "computer programme per se"

The Guidelines set out these steps:

  1. Understand the claimed invention as a whole, looking beyond the wording of the claims, and identify its essential technical features.
  2. Identify the core problem the invention addresses and the solution it proposes, and determine whether they are technical.
  3. Decide whether that technicality results in a technical effect that is beyond a mere incidental effect.
  4. If it does, the claim is not excluded as a computer programme per se; if it does not, it is excluded.

Substance over form

The Guidelines stress that what matters is the substance of the claim, not its form. Claims drafted as a method, a system or device, or a computer-readable medium are all excluded if, in substance, they fall within Section 3(k). Equally, a claim should not be refused if, taken as a whole, it does not fall within an excluded category. Claims only to a computer programme, a set of instructions, a "computer programme product" or a storage medium holding instructions are excluded.

The Guidelines also clarify that Section 3(k) does not restrict an application to only system claims or only method claims: both can be allowed, even as independent claims, if the specification supports them.

Business methods and mathematical methods

  • Business methods: the presence of words such as "business", "payment", "sales" or "transactions" in a claim does not by itself make it a business method. The question is where the core of the invention lies. If it is essentially about carrying out business, trade or financial activity, such as a financial scheme or a way of buying and selling goods online, it is excluded.
  • Mathematical methods: purely abstract calculation is excluded, but a mathematical formula in a claim does not automatically exclude it. Where the mathematics is part of a larger technical process, for example in encoding, noise reduction or encryption of electronic communications, the exclusion may not apply.

AI, blockchain and quantum computing

The 2025 Guidelines include a separate chapter on inventions involving artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, blockchain and quantum computing, with illustrative examples. The same principles apply: what counts is the technical problem, the technical solution and the technical effect, not the label.

Practical drafting tips

  • Describe the technical problem clearly, not just the business goal.
  • Explain how the solution works technically: data flows, system architecture, interactions with hardware, networks or sensors.
  • State the technical effect, such as faster processing, lower memory or bandwidth use, better security, or improved control of a device, with data where possible.
  • Give enough detail that a skilled person could implement the invention.
  • Do a prior art search first; a software-led invention must still be new and inventive.

Frequently asked questions

Can I patent a mobile app in India?

Not the app's code as such. If the app implements a technical solution to a technical problem with a technical effect, that underlying invention may be patentable. The code itself is protected by copyright.

Do I need new hardware to get a software-related patent?

No. The Delhi High Court in Raytheon and the CRI Guidelines 2025 both say novel hardware is not required.

Are business software inventions always refused?

No. A claim is excluded as a business method only if its core is a business or commercial method. Business context alone does not exclude it.
